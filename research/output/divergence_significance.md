# Is the divergence pattern statistically real?

Generated by `research/scripts/33_divergence_significance.py`. Two groups: the 621 rows where all four frontier models agree **and match** the gold label, and the 149 where they agree **and all four differ** from it.

## A correction to how these features were described

Earlier scripts called these "the criteria the guideline lists". That was wrong, and the error is worth stating plainly.

The official submission guidelines define the label in six characters -- *evidence definite, concretely credible* -- and give no enumeration of what counts as concrete. The seven patterns below are **this project's own operationalisation**, arrived at by observing what correlates with the label in the training data, not by transcribing a published rule.

This makes the under-determination argument stronger, not weaker: the guideline supplies **no operational criterion at all**, so a model given only the guideline text has nothing to apply. What follows is therefore a comparison against *our* notion of concreteness, and is labelled as such throughout.

| a named party | 0.335 | 0.268 | -0.066 | [-0.145, +0.014] | 0.12 | 0.12 |

**5 of 7 features survive Holm-Bonferroni correction at the 0.05 level.** Every surviving difference is negative: the rows the models get wrong together carry fewer of these markers.

## Reading

The pattern is not sampling noise. The differences are large, the intervals exclude zero, and they survive correction for testing seven features at once.

What that establishes is narrow but solid: **the paragraphs on which four independently built frontier models agree with each other and differ from the annotators are systematically the ones carrying fewer surface markers of concreteness.** It does not establish that the models are wrong, nor that the annotators are; script 31 addressed that separately and concluded that the guideline text does not determine the label either way.
