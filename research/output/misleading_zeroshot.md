## Reading

Both zero-shot routes for `Misleading` are now closed.

* Relatedness (script 09) put the five instances at cosine percentiles 54.9,
  10.8, 72.8, 8.5 and 90.7 -- one of them among the most related rows in the
  corpus.
* Definition-following, tested here with the guideline's own wording, catches
  **none of the five**. Four are called `Not Clear`, which is the semantically
  adjacent label, and one is called `Clear`.

The model does emit `Misleading` seven times, so it is not refusing the label; it
simply never agrees with the annotators about which rows deserve it. Given that
the guideline defines `Misleading` as evidence that "has no clear connection with
the claim and appears to divert attention", and that the same annotators reached
Krippendorff's alpha 0.940 on this field, the class is evidently learnable by
trained humans and not by any method available here without examples.

This matters for the metric, not for the leaderboard. With 3 `Misleading` rows in
the official test set, class F1 is `2*tp / (3 + predicted_count)`: flagging 30
rows to catch all 3 still yields only 0.18. Yet perfect prediction of those 3
rows is worth **+0.0875 on the official total**, against a 0.0248 spread across
all five of our competition submissions. A quarter of the `evidence_quality`
macro-F1, and 8.75 points of the overall score, rests on a class that neither a
fine-tuned ensemble, nor semantic relatedness, nor an instruction-tuned LLM given
the defining criterion can identify even once.

Incidentally the LLM massively over-predicts `Not Clear` (1191 against 451 gold),
which is the same failure direction the official RAG baseline shows on this field
(`evidence_quality` macro-F1 0.3599).
