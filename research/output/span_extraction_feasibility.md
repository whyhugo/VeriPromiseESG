## 2. Do spans align to sentence boundaries?

If they do, the task reduces from free-form character offsets to picking sentences, which is easier to model and to score.

| | count | share |
|---|---:|---:|
| every piece is exactly one whole sentence | 365 | 23% |
| sentence selection can reach char-F1 >= 0.95 | 537 | 34% |

**Ceiling of the sentence-selection reduction: char-F1 0.5414.** A model that only ever chose whole sentences could not do better, so sentences are the wrong unit.

### Why: the annotation is sub-sentence

| | value |
|---|---:|
| median sentence length | 66 chars |
| median promise piece length | 36 chars |
| pieces falling entirely inside one sentence | 2023 / 2202 (92%) |
| ...and the share of that sentence they occupy | median 0.41 |

Annotators marked a **clause**, not a sentence. Choosing whole sentences therefore over-selects by roughly two and a half times, which is exactly what caps the ceiling at 0.54.

### The right unit is the clause

Repeating the alignment test with the comma and enumeration mark added to the delimiters:

| unit | median length | span boundaries landing on a unit boundary | selection ceiling (char-F1) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| sentence (。！？；) | 66 chars | 24% | **0.5414** |
| **clause** (＋，、) | 14 chars | 71% | **0.9748** |

**At clause level the ceiling is 0.9748 and 71% of annotated boundaries fall exactly on a clause boundary.** That turns free-form character extraction into binary labelling over clauses -- is this clause part of the promise or not -- which is a standard, well-posed sequence-labelling problem with almost no headroom lost to the reduction.

## 4. Reading

The promise occupies about a quarter of its paragraph and starts a median of 8% of the way in, so neither "return everything" nor "return the first sentence" is close to right, which is what makes the task non-trivial.

**Sentences are the wrong unit and clauses are the right one.** Sentence selection caps at char-F1 0.5414 because annotators marked clauses, not sentences; at clause level the ceiling is 0.9748 with 71% of boundaries landing exactly. So the task should be posed as binary labelling over comma-delimited clauses, not as extractive question answering over characters.

A fifth to a quarter of the spans are several disjoint pieces, so this is discontinuous extraction rather than the single-span setting most extractive QA models assume. The clause-labelling framing handles that for free -- disjoint pieces are simply non-adjacent positive clauses -- whereas a start/end pointer model cannot represent them at all.

The best trivial baseline reaches char-F1 0.4803. That is what a trained clause labeller has to beat, against a ceiling of 0.9748.
